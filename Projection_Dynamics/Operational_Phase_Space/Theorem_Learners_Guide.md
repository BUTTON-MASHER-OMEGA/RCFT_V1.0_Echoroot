## The basic objects

**\(X\) or \(X_8\) — the eight-state carrier.** This is the set on which everything acts:

$$
\boxed{X_8=\{0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7\}.}
$$

Think of \(X_8\) as the collection of eight labeled positions/states. A permutation such as \(p,h,s\) rearranges these eight elements. At this stage, the labels \(0,\ldots,7\) have no physical meaning by themselves.

**\(p\) — the projection-pair involution.** In our frozen SIM14 realization,

$$
\boxed{p=(01)(23)(45)(67).}
$$

This means

$$
0\leftrightarrow1,\quad
2\leftrightarrow3,\quad
4\leftrightarrow5,\quad
6\leftrightarrow7.
$$

Applying \(p\) twice returns every state to itself:

$$
p^2=e.
$$

So \(p\) is an **involution**. In the RCFT toy architecture, \(p\) comes from the frozen projection pairing.

**\(h\) — the graph half-turn involution.** We have

$$
\boxed{h=(07)(16)(25)(34).}
$$

Thus

$$
0\leftrightarrow7,\quad
1\leftrightarrow6,\quad
2\leftrightarrow5,\quad
3\leftrightarrow4.
$$

Again,

$$
h^2=e.
$$

Unlike \(p\), \(h\) comes from the symmetry of the frozen \(C_8\) graph. So \(p\) and \(h\) are both involutions, but they enter the construction for different reasons.

---

## \(K\) — the group generated by \(p\) and \(h\)

We write

$$
\boxed{K=\langle p,h\rangle.}
$$

The angle brackets mean:

> Take \(p\) and \(h\), compose them in every possible way, and collect all distinct transformations that result.

Because

$$
p^2=h^2=e
$$

and

$$
ph=hp,
$$

we get only four transformations:

$$
\boxed{K=\{e,p,h,ph\}.}
$$

Here \(e\) means the identity transformation: do nothing.

The fourth element is

$$
ph=(06)(17)(24)(35).
$$

So

$$
|K|=4.
$$

Every nonidentity element has order two, and they commute. Therefore

$$
\boxed{K\cong V_4\cong\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_2.}
$$

This is the **Klein four group**.

A useful mental picture is therefore:

$$
p=\text{one binary direction},
$$

$$
h=\text{another independent binary direction},
$$

$$
ph=\text{doing both}.
$$

So before \(s\) ever appears, we already have a little two-bit transformation structure.

---

## \(k\) — an arbitrary element of \(K\)

Lowercase

$$
\boxed{k\in K}
$$

doesn't designate one special transformation.

It means **any element of \(K\)**.

Thus \(k\) could be

$$
e,\quad p,\quad h,\quad ph.
$$

This is why the theorem says

$$
sk=ks
\qquad\forall k\in K.
$$

It means \(s\) commutes not merely with one particular transformation but with **every transformation generated by \(p\) and \(h\)**.

Since \(p,h\) generate \(K\), checking

$$
sp=ps,\qquad sh=hs
$$

is sufficient.

---

## \(s\) — the additional involution

In the theorem,

$$
\boxed{s}
$$

is an additional transformation of \(X_8\) satisfying

$$
s^2=e,
$$

$$
sk=ks\qquad\forall k\in K,
$$

and

$$
s\notin K.
$$

So \(s\) supplies a **third independent binary transformation**.

Importantly, abstract \(s\) is not automatically the physical symplectic conjugation. In SIM14 we use \(s\) for candidate discrete realizations of the conjugate pairing supplied by the symplectic construction.

M1 and M2 are two examples.

---

## \(s_2\) — the M2 symplectic matching

Our important regular branch uses

$$
\boxed{s_2=(02)(13)(46)(57).}
$$

Thus

$$
0\leftrightarrow2,\quad
1\leftrightarrow3,\quad
4\leftrightarrow6,\quad
5\leftrightarrow7.
$$

Its underlying matching is

$$
M_2=\{0\!-\!2,1\!-\!3,4\!-\!6,5\!-\!7\}.
$$

SIM14.3 found that \(s_2\) commutes with \(p\) and \(h\), lies outside \(K\), and—critically—exchanges the two \(K\)-orbits.

That last property is what makes its action regular.

---

## \(sk\) — composition of \(s\) and \(k\)

The expression

$$
\boxed{sk}
$$

means: compose the transformations \(s\) and \(k\).

Under our convention, if acting on \(x\),

$$
(sk)(x)=s(k(x)).
$$

So first apply \(k\), then apply \(s\).

But because the theorem assumes

$$
sk=ks,
$$

we could equally write

$$
(sk)(x)=k(s(x)).
$$

This innocuous-looking equality is actually central to the proof.

It means \(s\) respects the entire organization created by \(K\).

---

# \(Kx\) — the \(K\)-orbit of a state

Take one state

$$
x\in X_8.
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{Kx=\{k(x):k\in K\}}
$$

means:

> Start at \(x\) and apply every transformation in \(K\). Collect all states you can reach.

For example, start at \(0\).

We have

$$
e(0)=0,
$$

$$
p(0)=1,
$$

$$
h(0)=7,
$$

and

$$
ph(0)=6.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{K0=\{0,1,6,7\}.}
$$

Start instead at \(2\):

$$
K2=\{2,3,4,5\}.
$$

These are the two **\(K\)-orbits**.

---

## \(O_0\) and \(O_1\)

Rather than repeatedly writing those sets, we name them:

$$
\boxed{O_0=\{0,1,6,7\}}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{O_1=\{2,3,4,5\}.}
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{X_8=O_0\sqcup O_1.}
$$

The symbol \(\sqcup\) means **disjoint union**: together the two sets contain all eight states, and they don't overlap.

These orbits are not something \(s_2\) creates.

They already exist under

$$
K=\langle p,h\rangle.
$$

That's crucial to understanding the theorem.

The story is:

$$
p,h
\longrightarrow
K
\longrightarrow
O_0\sqcup O_1,
$$

and only **then** do we ask what \(s\) does to those two existing pieces.

---

# Why \(s_2\) is an orbit bridge

Now apply \(s_2\) to \(O_0\):

$$
O_0=\{0,1,6,7\}.
$$

Using

$$
s_2=(02)(13)(46)(57),
$$

we get

$$
0\mapsto2,
\quad
1\mapsto3,
\quad
6\mapsto4,
\quad
7\mapsto5.
$$

Therefore

$$
s_2(O_0)=\{2,3,4,5\}=O_1.
$$

Likewise,

$$
s_2(O_1)=O_0.
$$

Hence

$$
\boxed{s_2(O_0)=O_1,\qquad s_2(O_1)=O_0.}
$$

That's exactly what we mean by an **orbit bridge**.

---

# \(B(s)\) — the orbit-transition matrix

Instead of describing that behavior verbally, SIM14.3 introduced

$$
\boxed{
B_{ab}(s)
=
\#\{x\in O_a:s(x)\in O_b\}.
}
$$

Read this as:

> Count how many states starting in orbit \(O_a\) are sent by \(s\) into orbit \(O_b\).

For \(s_2\), every one of the four states in \(O_0\) goes into \(O_1\), and every one of the four states in \(O_1\) goes into \(O_0\).

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
B(s_2)=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&4\\
4&0
\end{pmatrix}.
}
$$

The upper-left \(0\) means no states remain inside \(O_0\).

The upper-right \(4\) means all four states of \(O_0\) go to \(O_1\).

The lower-left \(4\) means all four states of \(O_1\) go to \(O_0\).

The lower-right \(0\) means none remain inside \(O_1\).

By comparison, M1 has

$$
B(s_1)=
\begin{pmatrix}
4&0\\
0&4
\end{pmatrix},
$$

because it preserves both orbits internally.

---

# \(G\) — add \(s\) to \(K\)

Now define

$$
\boxed{G=\langle K,s\rangle.}
$$

Since

$$
K=\langle p,h\rangle,
$$

this is equivalent to

$$
\boxed{G=\langle p,h,s\rangle.}
$$

For the M2 realization,

$$
G_2=\langle p,h,s_2\rangle.
$$

Because \(s_2\) is an involution, commutes with \(K\), and isn't already in \(K\),

$$
G_2\cong
K\times\langle s_2\rangle.
$$

Since

$$
K\cong(\mathbb Z_2)^2
$$

and

$$
\langle s_2\rangle\cong\mathbb Z_2,
$$

we get

$$
\boxed{
G_2\cong(\mathbb Z_2)^3.
}
$$

So we've gone from a two-bit transformation group

$$
K\cong(\mathbb Z_2)^2
$$

to a three-bit transformation group

$$
G_2\cong(\mathbb Z_2)^3.
$$

---

# “\(X\) is regular” — more precisely, \(G\) acts regularly on \(X\)

We should be precise here.

We don't really say

$$
X\text{ is regular}.
$$

We say

$$
\boxed{G\curvearrowright X\text{ is regular}.}
$$

The arrow means "\(G\) acts on \(X\)."

A **regular action** has two properties:

$$
\text{transitive}+\text{free}.
$$

**Transitive** means every state can be reached from every other state using some element of \(G\).

**Free** means no nonidentity group element leaves any state fixed.

Together they imply something very strong:

> For every ordered pair \(x,y\in X\), there is exactly one \(g\in G\) such that \(g(x)=y\).

For M2,

$$
|G_2|=8
$$

and

$$
|X_8|=8.
$$

Because \(s_2\) bridges \(O_0\) and \(O_1\), \(G_2\) becomes transitive across all eight states. Orbit-stabilizer then forces the stabilizer of every state to contain only the identity.

Hence

$$
\boxed{G_2\curvearrowright X_8\text{ is regular}.}
$$

---

# \(\mathbb F_2^3\) — our affine torsor

Now comes the payoff.

$$
\boxed{\mathbb F_2=\{0,1\}}
$$

is the field containing two elements, with arithmetic modulo \(2\).

Then

$$
\mathbb F_2^3
$$

contains the eight binary triples

$$
000,\;001,\;010,\;011,\;100,\;101,\;110,\;111.
$$

As an additive group,

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb F_2^3
\cong
(\mathbb Z_2)^3
\cong
G_2.
}
$$

Since \(G_2\) acts regularly on \(X_8\), we can identify the **transformations** in \(G_2\) with binary translations.

But there's a subtlety.

There is no state in \(X_8\) intrinsically marked as

$$
000.
$$

We have to choose one.

Suppose we choose

$$
0\in X_8
$$

as our origin and call it \(000\).

Then every group element sends that origin to exactly one microscopic state. Because the action is regular, this establishes a one-to-one correspondence:

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb F_2^3
\longleftrightarrow
X_8.
}
$$

That makes \(X_8\) an **affine \(\mathbb F_2^3\)-torsor**.

“Torsor” is basically the right mathematical phrase for:

> It behaves exactly like the vector space under translations, except that the carrier itself doesn't intrinsically tell us which point is zero.

Once an origin is chosen, it looks like ordinary \(\mathbb F_2^3\).

---

## How \(p,h,s_2\) become the three binary directions

With a chosen basis we can assign, for example,

$$
p\leftrightarrow100,
$$

$$
h\leftrightarrow010,
$$

$$
s_2\leftrightarrow001.
$$

Then compositions become binary addition:

$$
ph\leftrightarrow110,
$$

$$
ps_2\leftrightarrow101,
$$

$$
hs_2\leftrightarrow011,
$$

$$
phs_2\leftrightarrow111.
$$

And the identity is

$$
e\leftrightarrow000.
$$

So the entire group becomes

$$
\boxed{
G_2=
\{
e,p,h,s_2,ph,ps_2,hs_2,phs_2
\}
\cong
\mathbb F_2^3.
}
$$

This is the precise origin of our **seven binary directions**:

$$
001,\;010,\;011,\;100,\;101,\;110,\;111.
$$

They're simply the seven nonzero transformations.

## The map \(\Phi:G\to X,\;g\mapsto g(x_0)\)

Once a group \(G\) acts **regularly** on a set \(X\), choose one point

$$
x_0\in X.
$$

We then define

$$
\boxed{
\Phi:G\longrightarrow X,
\qquad
g\longmapsto g(x_0).
}
$$

In words:

> Pick one state \(x_0\) as a reference point. For every transformation \(g\in G\), ask where \(g\) sends that reference state.

In our M2 realization,

$$
G=\langle p,h,s_2\rangle
\cong(\mathbb Z_2)^3,
$$

and

$$
X=X_8.
$$

Suppose we choose

$$
x_0=0.
$$

Then

$$
\Phi(e)=0,
$$

because the identity leaves \(0\) unchanged.

Likewise,

$$
\Phi(p)=p(0)=1,
$$

$$
\Phi(h)=h(0)=7,
$$

and

$$
\Phi(s_2)=s_2(0)=2.
$$

Continuing with compositions gives all eight microscopic states.

Using the basis assignment

$$
p\leftrightarrow100,\qquad
h\leftrightarrow010,\qquad
s_2\leftrightarrow001,
$$

we recover the SIM14.1 coordinate assignment:

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
g & \mathbb F_2^3 & \Phi(g)=g(0)\\
\hline
e & 000 & 0\\
p & 100 & 1\\
h & 010 & 7\\
s_2 & 001 & 2\\
ph & 110 & 6\\
ps_2 & 101 & 3\\
hs_2 & 011 & 5\\
phs_2 & 111 & 4
\end{array}
$$

Hence the corresponding carrier coordinates are

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
0&\leftrightarrow000,\\
1&\leftrightarrow100,\\
2&\leftrightarrow001,\\
3&\leftrightarrow101,\\
4&\leftrightarrow111,\\
5&\leftrightarrow011,\\
6&\leftrightarrow110,\\
7&\leftrightarrow010.
\end{aligned}}
$$

### Why \(\Phi\) is a bijection

Regularity gives us exactly what we need.

**Transitivity** says that for every \(x\in X\), some \(g\in G\) satisfies

$$
g(x_0)=x.
$$

Therefore \(\Phi\) is **surjective**.

**Freeness** says that if

$$
g(x_0)=g'(x_0),
$$

then

$$
g^{-1}g'(x_0)=x_0.
$$

Since only the identity can fix \(x_0\),

$$
g^{-1}g'=e,
$$

and therefore

$$
g=g'.
$$

So \(\Phi\) is **injective**. (crucial for determinism)

Together,

$$
\boxed{\Phi:G\xrightarrow{\sim}X}
$$

is a bijection.

This is the mathematical reason the eight group elements can become coordinates for the eight microscopic states.

But there's an important subtlety:

$$
\Phi
$$

depends on our choice of \(x_0\).

Nothing in the bare torsor says that state \(0\) is inherently the origin. We chose it.

That's why \(X_8\) is more precisely an **affine torsor** rather than a canonically based copy of \(\mathbb F_2^3\).

## Orbit-stabilizer

The equation

$$
\boxed{
|G|
=
|\operatorname{Orb}_G(x)|
\,|\operatorname{Stab}_G(x)|
}
$$

is the **orbit-stabilizer theorem**.

It connects three quantities.

The **group**

$$
G
$$

contains every allowed transformation.

The **orbit**

$$
\operatorname{Orb}_G(x)
=
\{g(x):g\in G\}
$$

contains every state reachable from \(x\).

The **stabilizer**

$$
\operatorname{Stab}_G(x)
=
\{g\in G:g(x)=x\}
$$

contains every transformation that leaves \(x\) fixed.

So orbit-stabilizer says:

> Group size = number of distinct places \(x\) can be sent × number of transformations producing each destination degeneracy at \(x\).

That interpretation is especially useful for M1 versus M2.

### M2

For M2,

$$
|G_2|=8.
$$

Its orbit contains all eight states:

$$
|\operatorname{Orb}_{G_2}(x)|=8.
$$

Therefore

$$
8=8\,
|\operatorname{Stab}_{G_2}(x)|.
$$

Hence

$$
\boxed{
|\operatorname{Stab}_{G_2}(x)|=1.
}
$$

The only transformation fixing \(x\) is the identity.

So M2 is free and transitive:

$$
\boxed{\text{regular}.}
$$

SIM14.3 directly measured exactly this: M2 had one eight-state orbit and stabilizer size \(1\) at every state. 

### M1

M1 is quite different.

We still have

$$
|G_1|=8.
$$

But a state can only explore one four-state orbit:

$$
|\operatorname{Orb}_{G_1}(x)|=4.
$$

Therefore

$$
8=4\,
|\operatorname{Stab}_{G_1}(x)|.
$$

So

$$
\boxed{
|\operatorname{Stab}_{G_1}(x)|=2.
}
$$

That means there is a nonidentity group element fixing each state.

Hence M1 isn't free.

And because its orbit contains only four of the eight states, it isn't transitive either.

Therefore

$$
\boxed{\text{M1 is nonregular}.}
$$

SIM14.3 measured precisely these values: M1 gives orbit structure \(4+4\) and stabilizer size \(2\) everywhere. 

So orbit-stabilizer exposes the bifurcation beautifully:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
 & |G| & |\operatorname{Orb}(x)| & |\operatorname{Stab}(x)|\\
\hline
M1 &8&4&2\\
M2 &8&8&1
\end{array}}
$$

The abstract groups have the same size.

The difference is **how that group acts on the carrier**.

## \(x\mapsto x+d,\;d\in\mathbb F_2^3\)

Once M2 gives us a regular action, we may choose an origin and identify

$$
X_8\cong\mathbb F_2^3
$$

as an affine torsor.

Then every group element can be interpreted as a **translation by a binary displacement**:

$$
\boxed{
x\longmapsto x+d,
\qquad
d\in\mathbb F_2^3.
}
$$

Addition is componentwise modulo two.

For example,

$$
x=101,
\qquad
d=011.
$$

Then

$$
101+011=110.
$$

Because

$$
1+1=0
$$

in \(\mathbb F_2\).

So the translation

$$
d=011
$$

takes

$$
101\mapsto110.
$$

Apply the same translation again:

$$
110+011=101.
$$

Therefore

$$
(x+d)+d=x.
$$

Algebraically,

$$
d+d=000.
$$

This explains why every nonzero translation is an involution.

### Our generators

Under our chosen basis,

$$
p\leftrightarrow100,
$$

so \(p\) means

$$
\boxed{x\mapsto x+100.}
$$

Similarly,

$$
h\leftrightarrow010
$$

means

$$
\boxed{x\mapsto x+010,}
$$

and

$$
s_2\leftrightarrow001
$$

means

$$
\boxed{x\mapsto x+001.}
$$

Their compositions become vector addition.

For example,

$$
ps_2
\leftrightarrow
100+001
=
101.
$$

And

$$
phs_2
\leftrightarrow
100+010+001
=
111.
$$

So composition of transformations has become ordinary addition in

$$
\mathbb F_2^3.
$$

That's the deep convenience of the torsor representation.

## \(G\setminus\{e\}\)

The notation

$$
\boxed{G\setminus\{e\}}
$$

means:

> Take the group \(G\) and remove its identity element \(e\).

For our regular M2 group,

$$
G\cong(\mathbb Z_2)^3
$$

contains eight elements:

$$
G=
\{
e,p,h,s_2,ph,ps_2,hs_2,phs_2
\}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
G\setminus\{e\}
=
\{
p,h,s_2,ph,ps_2,hs_2,phs_2
\}.
}
$$

There are exactly seven.

Under the binary representation,

$$
e\leftrightarrow000.
$$

So

$$
G\setminus\{e\}
$$

corresponds to

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb F_2^3\setminus\{000\}.
}
$$

Explicitly,

$$
\{
001,010,011,100,101,110,111
\}.
$$

These are what we've been calling the **seven binary directions**.

Why "directions"?

Because each one defines a nontrivial translation

$$
x\mapsto x+d.
$$

For example,

$$
d=001
$$

defines one direction,

$$
d=010
$$

another, and

$$
d=011
$$

another.

Over \(\mathbb F_2\), there is no distinction between a nonzero vector and its scalar multiples because the only nonzero scalar is \(1\).

That's precisely why these seven nonzero vectors correspond directly to the seven projective points of

$$
PG(2,2).
$$

So we now have the clean chain

$$
\boxed{
G\setminus\{e\}
\leftrightarrow
\mathbb F_2^3\setminus\{0\}
\leftrightarrow
7\text{ binary directions}
\leftrightarrow
7\text{ points of }PG(2,2).
}
$$

That last correspondence is standard finite geometry; RCFT isn't inventing it.

## The M1/M2 regularity bifurcation after SIM14.3

This is probably the most important interpretive cleanup.

### Before SIM14.2

M2 gave us

$$
\langle p,h,s_2\rangle
\cong
(\mathbb Z_2)^3
$$

acting regularly on \(X_8\).

That looked potentially like the important fact.

Then the seven nonidentity elements gave seven directions and seven \(V_4\) subgroups, producing

$$
PG(2,2).
$$

A tempting interpretation would have been:

> Adding a \(C_8\)-compatible canonical symplectic conjugation produces the eight-state Fano/torsor organization.

SIM14.2 killed that claim.

M1 is also \(C_8\)-contained and also gives

$$
\boxed{
G_1\cong(\mathbb Z_2)^3.
}
$$

But it doesn't act regularly.

So the abstract group alone cannot explain the microscopic organization.

---

## After SIM14.3, we know exactly where the bifurcation occurs

Before either \(s_1\) or \(s_2\) enters, we already have

$$
K=\langle p,h\rangle\cong V_4
$$

acting as

$$
\boxed{
X_8=O_0\sqcup O_1
}
$$

with

$$
O_0=\{0,1,6,7\},
\qquad
O_1=\{2,3,4,5\}.
$$

So the carrier has a pre-existing

$$
4+4
$$

organization.

Now compare the two symplectic embeddings.

### M1 stays inside the existing sectors

$$
s_1=(01)(24)(35)(67).
$$

It satisfies

$$
\boxed{
s_1(O_0)=O_0,
\qquad
s_1(O_1)=O_1.
}
$$

So adding \(s_1\) enlarges the **abstract transformation group**:

$$
V_4
\rightarrow
(\mathbb Z_2)^3,
$$

but it does **not** connect the two existing \(K\)-orbits.

Consequently,

$$
X_8
$$

remains

$$
4+4.
$$

This is a really interesting distinction:

$$
\boxed{
\text{algebraic enlargement without carrier unification}.
}
$$

M1 adds an independent group generator, but that generator operates internally relative to the existing orbit decomposition.

---

## M2 bridges the existing sectors

M2 gives

$$
s_2=(02)(13)(46)(57).
$$

Now

$$
\boxed{
s_2(O_0)=O_1,
\qquad
s_2(O_1)=O_0.
}
$$

So M2 also enlarges

$$
V_4
\rightarrow
(\mathbb Z_2)^3,
$$

but its new generator does something qualitatively different to the carrier:

$$
\boxed{\text{it connects the two previously disconnected group orbits}.}
$$

The resulting action becomes

$$
8
$$

rather than

$$
4+4.
$$

And because

$$
|G_2|=|X_8|=8,
$$

that transitive action becomes regular.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{algebraic enlargement + orbit bridging}
\Longrightarrow
\text{carrier unification}.
}
$$

Here "carrier unification" is useful descriptive SIM terminology, not a physical unification claim.

## What changed conceptually

We originally had three things that were easy to blur together:

$$
\text{group structure},
\qquad
\text{group action},
\qquad
\text{carrier geometry}.
$$

SIM14.3 separates them.

Both M1 and M2 can give the same abstract algebra:

$$
\boxed{G_1\cong G_2\cong(\mathbb Z_2)^3.}
$$

But

$$
\boxed{
G_1\curvearrowright X_8
\not\cong
G_2\curvearrowright X_8
}
$$

as permutation actions, because their orbit structures differ.

M1:

$$
8\rightarrow4+4.
$$

M2:

$$
8\rightarrow8.
$$

So the emergent torsor structure does **not** come merely from possessing three commuting involutions.

It comes from the relationship between the third involution and the orbit structure already established by the first two.

That gives us a much sharper statement:

$$
\boxed{
\text{The decisive object is not merely }s;
\text{ it is }s\text{ relative to the }K\text{-action}.
}
$$

Or, even more compactly,

$$
\boxed{
\text{structure resides in the relation between operations, not merely in the operations individually.}
}
$$

That final sentence is mathematically justified **at the level of this finite carrier**. I would resist turning it into a general RCFT ontological principle just yet—but it is certainly consonant with the relational direction of the theory.

And SIM14.3 goes one step beyond the numerical observation: within the commuting class it found the exact discriminator

$$
\boxed{
B(s)=
\begin{pmatrix}0&4\\4&0\end{pmatrix}
\iff
\text{regular action},
}
$$

with all four bridge candidates regular and all nine non-bridge candidates nonregular. 

So M1/M2 is no longer an unexplained bifurcation. **The bifurcation variable is orbit bridging.**

$$
\boxed{
\begin{array}{ccccc}
p&&h\\
\searrow&&\swarrow\\
&K=\langle p,h\rangle\cong V_4&\\
&\downarrow&\\
&O_0\sqcup O_1&\\

And then, one step farther downstream,

$$
\mathbb F_2^3
\longrightarrow
7\text{ nonzero binary directions}

$$

So the conceptual core of the theorem is surprisingly simple:

$$
\boxed{
\underbrace{p,h}_{\text{build two }4\text{-state orbits}}
\quad+\quad
\underbrace{s_2}_{\text{bridges those orbits}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\underbrace{\text{regular 8-state action}}_{\text{affine }\mathbb F_2^3\text{ torsor}}.
}
$$

That is what all of the notation is saying.
