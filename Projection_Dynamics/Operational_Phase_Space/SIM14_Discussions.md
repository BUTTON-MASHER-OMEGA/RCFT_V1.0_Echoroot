$$
[s,k]=e
\qquad\forall k\in K.
$$

Then, provided \(s\notin K\),

$$
G=\langle K,s\rangle\cong(\mathbb Z_2)^3.
$$

Moreover,

$$
\boxed{
G\curvearrowright X\text{ is regular}
\iff
s(O_0)=O_1.
}
$$

And the proof looks almost embarrassingly short now.

Because \(s\) commutes with \(K\), if \(s\) sends **one** \(x\in O_0\) into \(O_1\), then for every \(k\in K\),

$$
s(kx)=k\,s(x).
$$

But \(Kx=O_0\), while \(Ks(x)=O_1\). Therefore

$$
s(O_0)=O_1.
$$

So commutation itself explains why SIM14.3 found no intermediate matrices like

$$
\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

That's an important little deduction from the output: **the all-or-nothing bridging behavior isn't a numerical accident.** It's forced by \(K\)-equivariance.

If \(s\) preserves \(O_0\), then \(\langle K,s\rangle\) cannot be transitive on \(X\), because both \(K\) and \(s\) preserve the two-orbit decomposition.

Conversely, if \(s\) exchanges \(O_0\) and \(O_1\), then \(K\) moves any point to every point within its four-state orbit, while \(s\) moves between the two orbits. Hence \(\langle K,s\rangle\) is transitive on all eight points.

Since \(s\notin K\), commutation and \(s^2=e\) give

$$
|\langle K,s\rangle|=8.
$$

A transitive action of a group of order eight on a set of eight elements has stabilizers of size

$$
\frac{|G|}{|X|}
=
\frac88=1
$$

by orbit-stabilizer.

Therefore the action is regular.

That's basically the theorem.

### And C8 now has a very precise role

This may be my favorite part.

The orbit-bridge law **is not caused by \(C_8\)**.

Across the whole commuting class, there are four regular bridge candidates. Three don't live entirely on the C8 edges. 

So we should write:

$$
\boxed{
K+s\text{ determines regularity;}
\qquad
C_8\text{ restricts admissibility.}
}
$$

And under that additional \(C_8\)-containment condition, only two candidates survive:

$$
M_1,\quad M_2.
$$

Of those,

$$
M_1:\text{ internal/nonregular},
$$

$$
M_2:\text{ bridging/regular}.
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\text{within }S_K\text{ and the frozen }C_8,
\quad M_2\text{ is the unique regular matching.}
}
$$

That's exactly what the run reports. 

Notice the careful wording: **unique under the frozen mathematical constraints we've actually tested**, not "physically selected."

That distinction remains essential.

## This also explains SIM14.1 much better

Our story has changed quite a lot over three SIMs.

\rightarrow

\rightarrow

Now we know the deeper structure is:

with two possible qualitative relationships:

That's much cleaner than where we started.

And there's another satisfying piece: all ten commuting candidates outside \(K\) generated order-eight groups whose elements have order at most two.  So the difference between M1 and M2 was **never the abstract group**.

That feels like one of the most useful conceptual lessons of SIM14 so far.
