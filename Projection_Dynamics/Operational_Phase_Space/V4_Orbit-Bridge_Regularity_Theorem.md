# \(V_4\) Orbit-Bridge Regularity Theorem

### Theorem

Let \(X\) be an eight-element set, and let

$$
K\cong V_4\cong \mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_2
$$

act freely on \(X\). Suppose the \(K\)-action has exactly two orbits,

$$
X=O_0\sqcup O_1,
\qquad
|O_0|=|O_1|=4.
$$

Let

$$
s\in\operatorname{Sym}(X)
$$

be an involution satisfying

$$
s^2=e,
$$

such that \(s\) commutes with the action of \(K\):

$$
sk=ks
\qquad
\forall k\in K.
$$

Assume further that

$$
s\notin K.
$$

Define

$$
G=\langle K,s\rangle.
$$

Then:

1. \(G\cong K\times\langle s\rangle\cong(\mathbb Z_2)^3\), and hence \(|G|=8\).

2. The map \(s\) either preserves both \(K\)-orbits or exchanges them completely:

$$
s(O_i)=O_i\quad(i=0,1),
$$

or

$$
s(O_0)=O_1,\qquad s(O_1)=O_0.
$$

No partial exchange is possible.

3. The action \(G\curvearrowright X\) is regular **if and only if** \(s\) exchanges the two \(K\)-orbits:

$$
\boxed{
G\curvearrowright X\text{ regular}
\iff
s(O_0)=O_1.
}
$$

Equivalently, if the orbit-transition matrix is defined by

$$
B_{ab}(s)
=
\#\{x\in O_a:s(x)\in O_b\},
$$

then

$$
\boxed{
G\curvearrowright X\text{ regular}
\iff
B(s)=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&4\\
4&0
\end{pmatrix}.
}
$$

If instead

$$
B(s)=
\begin{pmatrix}
4&0\\
0&4
\end{pmatrix},
$$

then \(G\) is not transitive on \(X\), and its orbit decomposition remains

$$
X=O_0\sqcup O_1.
$$

---

# Proof

Because

$$
K\cong V_4,
$$

we have

$$
|K|=4,
$$

and every nonidentity element of \(K\) has order two.

Since \(s^2=e\), the subgroup generated by \(s\) is

$$
\langle s\rangle=\{e,s\}\cong\mathbb Z_2.
$$

By hypothesis,

$$
sk=ks
\qquad\forall k\in K,
$$

so \(K\) and \(\langle s\rangle\) commute elementwise.

Furthermore,

$$
s\notin K.
$$

Because \(\langle s\rangle=\{e,s\}\), it follows that

$$
K\cap\langle s\rangle=\{e\}.
$$

Therefore

$$
G=\langle K,s\rangle
=
K\langle s\rangle
\cong
K\times\langle s\rangle.
$$

Hence

$$
G
\cong
(\mathbb Z_2)^2\times\mathbb Z_2
\cong
(\mathbb Z_2)^3,
$$

and

$$
\boxed{|G|=8.}
$$

This proves the first assertion.

---

## Lemma 1 — \(s\) maps a \(K\)-orbit onto a \(K\)-orbit

Choose any \(x\in X\).

Its \(K\)-orbit is

$$
Kx=\{k(x):k\in K\}.
$$

Apply \(s\):

$$
s(Kx)
=
\{s(k(x)):k\in K\}.
$$

Since \(s\) commutes with every \(k\in K\),

$$
s(k(x))=k(s(x)).
$$

Therefore

$$
s(Kx)
=
\{k(s(x)):k\in K\}
=
K(s(x)).
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{s(Kx)=K(s(x)).}
$$

So \(s\) maps every complete \(K\)-orbit onto another complete \(K\)-orbit.

Because the \(K\)-action has exactly the two orbits \(O_0,O_1\), we must therefore have

$$
s(O_i)\in\{O_0,O_1\}.
$$

Since \(s\) is a bijection, the induced action of \(s\) on the two-element orbit set

$$
X/K=\{O_0,O_1\}
$$

is itself a permutation.

There are only two possibilities:

$$
O_0\mapsto O_0,\qquad O_1\mapsto O_1,
$$

or

$$
O_0\mapsto O_1,\qquad O_1\mapsto O_0.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{\text{partial orbit exchange is impossible}.}
$$

In particular, matrices such as

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
2&2\\
2&2
\end{pmatrix}
$$

cannot occur under the hypotheses of the theorem.

This explains analytically the all-or-nothing structure observed in SIM14.3.

---

# Forward direction

Suppose

$$
G\curvearrowright X
$$

is regular.

A regular action is, in particular, transitive.

Assume for contradiction that \(s\) preserves the \(K\)-orbits:

$$
s(O_0)=O_0,
\qquad
s(O_1)=O_1.
$$

Every element of \(K\) also preserves each \(K\)-orbit by definition. Therefore every generator of

$$
G=\langle K,s\rangle
$$

preserves \(O_0\) and \(O_1\).

It follows that every element of \(G\) preserves them.

Hence no element of \(G\) can send a point of \(O_0\) to a point of \(O_1\).

Thus the \(G\)-action cannot be transitive.

This contradicts regularity.

Therefore regularity requires orbit exchange:

$$
\boxed{
G\curvearrowright X\text{ regular}
\Longrightarrow
s(O_0)=O_1.
}
$$

---

# Reverse direction

Now suppose

$$
s(O_0)=O_1.
$$

Because \(s^2=e\),

$$
s(O_1)=O_0.
$$

We first prove that \(G\) acts transitively on \(X\).

Take arbitrary

$$
x,y\in X.
$$

If \(x\) and \(y\) lie in the same \(K\)-orbit, then because the \(K\)-action on each \(O_i\) is transitive, there exists

$$
k\in K
$$

such that

$$
k(x)=y.
$$

Now suppose \(x\) and \(y\) lie in different \(K\)-orbits.

Since \(s\) exchanges the two orbits,

$$
s(x)
$$

lies in the same \(K\)-orbit as \(y\).

Therefore there exists

$$
k\in K
$$

such that

$$
k(s(x))=y.
$$

Thus

$$
(ks)(x)=y.
$$

So in either case some element of \(G\) sends \(x\) to \(y\).

Hence

$$
G\curvearrowright X
$$

is transitive.

But we already proved

$$
|G|=8,
$$

while by hypothesis

$$
|X|=8.
$$

For any \(x\in X\), the orbit-stabilizer theorem gives

$$
|G|
=
|\operatorname{Orb}_G(x)|
\,|\operatorname{Stab}_G(x)|.
$$

Transitivity gives

$$
|\operatorname{Orb}_G(x)|=8.
$$

Therefore

$$
8
=
8\,|\operatorname{Stab}_G(x)|,
$$

so

$$
|\operatorname{Stab}_G(x)|=1.
$$

Thus every stabilizer is trivial.

The action is therefore free as well as transitive and hence regular:

$$
\boxed{
s(O_0)=O_1
\Longrightarrow
G\curvearrowright X\text{ regular}.
}
$$

Combining both directions,

$$
\boxed{
G\curvearrowright X\text{ regular}
\iff
s(O_0)=O_1.
}
$$

This proves the theorem. \(\square\)

# Corollary 1 — Orbit-transition classification

Under the hypotheses of the theorem, there are only two possible orbit-transition matrices:

$$
\boxed{
B(s)\in
\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}
4&0\\
0&4
\end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix}
0&4\\
4&0
\end{pmatrix}
\right\}.
}
$$

Moreover,

$$
B(s)=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&4\\
4&0
\end{pmatrix}
\iff
G\curvearrowright X
\text{ is regular}.
$$

So the \(B(s)\) classification seen computationally in SIM14.3 is not a sampling pattern. It follows from equivariance of \(s\) with respect to \(K\).

# Corollary 2 — Affine \(\mathbb F_2^3\) torsor

If \(s\) exchanges the two \(K\)-orbits, then

$$
G\cong(\mathbb Z_2)^3
$$

acts regularly on \(X\).

Therefore \(X\) is a \(G\)-torsor.

After choosing an arbitrary base point

$$
x_0\in X,
$$

the map

$$
\Phi:G\rightarrow X,
\qquad
g\mapsto g(x_0)
$$

is a bijection.

Identifying

$$
G\cong\mathbb F_2^3
$$

therefore gives

$$
\boxed{
X\cong\mathbb F_2^3
}
$$

as an affine torsor—not as a canonically based vector space.

This distinction is important: the theorem does not naturally select which microscopic state should be called

$$
000.
$$

An origin must be chosen.

Once it is chosen, the group action becomes translation:

$$
\boxed{x\mapsto x+d,\qquad d\in\mathbb F_2^3.}
$$

That supplies the precise mathematical foundation for the torsor coordinates used in SIM14.1.

# Corollary 3 — Seven binary directions and \(PG(2,2)\)

The regular case gives

$$
G\cong\mathbb F_2^3.
$$

There are exactly seven nonzero elements

$$
G\setminus\{e\}.
$$

Each determines a nontrivial translation of \(X\).

Projectivizing the three-dimensional vector space over \(\mathbb F_2\) yields

$$
PG(2,2),
$$

the Fano plane.

Because the only nonzero scalar in \(\mathbb F_2\) is \(1\), its seven projective points correspond directly to the seven nonzero vectors.

Its seven projective lines are the two-dimensional subspaces

$$
\{0,a,b,a+b\},
$$

whose nonidentity elements are

$$
\{a,b,a+b\}.
$$

As additive groups these four-element subspaces are

$$
V_4.
$$

Hence:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
7\text{ nonidentity elements of }G
&\longleftrightarrow
7\text{ points of }PG(2,2),\\
7\text{ order-four }V_4\text{ subgroups}
&\longleftrightarrow
7\text{ lines of }PG(2,2).
\end{aligned}}
$$

This is a standard consequence of the regular \(\mathbb F_2^3\) structure. It is **not an additional RCFT theorem**.

# One assumption we can actually weaken

There's a nice cleanup worth recording.

SIM14.3 enumerated **fixed-point-free** involutions \(s\). But the theorem's regular branch doesn't really need fixed-point-freeness as an independent assumption.

If

$$
s(O_0)=O_1,
$$

then for every \(x\in X\),

$$
s(x)
$$

lies in the other orbit and therefore

$$
s(x)\neq x.
$$

So complete orbit exchange automatically makes \(s\) fixed-point-free.

That means the core theorem can be formulated using only:

$$
\boxed{
s^2=e,\qquad
[s,K]=e,\qquad
s\notin K.
}
$$

The fixed-point-free condition belongs naturally to the **SIM14 symplectic-matching candidate class**, but isn't logically necessary for the bridge \(\Rightarrow\) regular part of the theorem.

# What \(C_8\) does—and does not—do

The theorem contains no \(C_8\).

That's important.

We discovered it using the symplectic \(C_8\) laboratory, but the theorem is fundamentally about

$$
\boxed{
V_4\text{ acting freely as }4+4
+
\text{a commuting independent involution}.
}
$$

So we should **not** call this a \(C_8\) theorem.

Within RCFT, \(C_8\) enters one layer earlier/later as an admissibility condition on which conjugate matchings are available.

Thus the mathematical architecture is now more precisely:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{array}{ccccc}
&&K\cong V_4&&\\
&&\curvearrowright&&\\
&&O_0\sqcup O_1&&\\
&&\downarrow s&&\\
&\swarrow&&\searrow&\\
\text{orbit preserving}&&&&\text{orbit exchanging}\\
\downarrow&&&&\downarrow\\
4+4&&&&8\\
\text{nontransitive}&&&&\text{regular}\\
&&&&\downarrow\\
&&&&\mathbb F_2^3\text{ torsor}\\
&&&&\downarrow\\
&&&&PG(2,2)
\end{array}
}
$$

while

$$
C_8
$$

determines which candidate pairings survive the frozen adjacency constraint.

That is substantially sharper than saying "\(C_8\) generates the Fano plane."

It doesn't.

## Formal status after the proof

I would now update the SIM14 truth ledger to three separate levels:

**Mathematically proved:** Under the theorem's assumptions, a commuting independent involution produces a regular \((\mathbb Z_2)^3\) action on the eight-element carrier **if** it exchanges the two free \(V_4\) orbits. Complete versus partial exchange is forced to be all-or-nothing by \(K\)-equivariance.

**Computationally verified in RCFT's frozen carrier:** SIM14.3 exhaustively checked all 105 perfect matchings, found the 13-member commuting class \(S_K\), and found no counterexample to the theorem's criterion; M1 is orbit-preserving and M2 orbit-exchanging.

**Still RCFT hypothesis:** Why the physically relevant construction, if any, should select M2; whether the resulting torsor/Fano organization connects to the hypothesized Fano/octonionic, \(RE_8/4_{21}\), \(H_4\), ISP/LCO, spinorial, or physical structures.

And that last boundary matters a lot. We've now eliminated one thing from the hypothesis pile and placed it firmly in the mathematics pile:

$$
\boxed{
\textbf{The M1/M2 regularity bifurcation is no longer merely a SIM observation.}
}
$$

Its mechanism is a straightforward consequence of group actions.
